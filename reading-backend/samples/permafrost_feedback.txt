The permafrost carbon feedback describes a mechanism by which climate warming accelerates itself, independent of further human emissions. The process begins as rising Arctic air temperatures warm the uppermost layers of permafrost soil, causing previously frozen ground to thaw for the first time in millennia. Thawing exposes organic carbon that has been locked in the soil since the last glacial period, some of it tens of thousands of years old. Once exposed to oxygen and liquid water, this organic matter becomes available to soil microbes, which metabolise it and release carbon dioxide and methane as by-products. Where thaw occurs in waterlogged, low-oxygen conditions, microbial decomposition favours methane production over carbon dioxide; because methane traps roughly eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide over a twenty-year horizon, the composition of the thaw environment substantially changes the strength of the feedback. These greenhouse gases then enter the atmosphere, adding to the existing warming trend caused by anthropogenic emissions. The added atmospheric carbon further raises global and regional temperatures, which in turn drives deeper and more extensive permafrost thaw the following season. Schuur et al. (2015) describe this as a slow but effectively irreversible feedback on human timescales: unlike emissions from fossil fuel combustion, which can in principle be curtailed by policy, microbial decomposition of thawed soil carbon continues regardless of future mitigation efforts once triggered. Estimates of the total carbon stock vulnerable to this process vary considerably, but most place it at more than twice the carbon currently in the atmosphere, which is what makes the feedback a focus of concern even though its year-to-year contribution to warming remains smaller than direct anthropogenic emissions for now.
